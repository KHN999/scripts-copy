There is nothing supernatural in this film, and the villain must never look like one.

1. Daw Than Mya is warm in every single frame. No snarl, no looming, no under-lighting, no wild eyes, no blood on her — not even in 45, where she is holding a cleaver and should look like a woman about to start a chore. The only shift is 32, where the warmth simply drains out of her face into something flat and honest. Everything frightening about her is in what she is saying. If a shot makes her look evil, the film stops working, because the whole point is that the village was right to trust her and also completely wrong.

2. Her kindness is real, so shoot it straight. Shots 1, 2, 6, 11, 20 are genuine generosity — she is not pretending. Shot 29 repeats that same gesture after the reveal, unchanged, so the audience re-reads it themselves.

3. Almost no gore. The chopping board in 18 is ordinary butchery — cuts of meat, a little blood, nothing anatomical or identifiable. The dig in 58 shows one old bone and some fragments on a tarpaulin, shot from a distance. That is the entire budget; there is nothing else in the film.

4. Two objects carry the plot, and neither owner is ever shown. A steel wristwatch with a worn brown leather strap appears on the kitchen floor in 19 and in Zaw Min's hand in 23 — generate it once and reuse the same watch for both, because the audience has to recognise it. Likewise the patterned blouse left on the line in 14. The two missing workers exist only as these objects; there is no reference to build for them.

5. Nothing readable, anywhere. The story runs on documents: the ID cards in the chest (22), the writing on the back of the photograph (25), the list of names (27), the phone screens (42, 54), the clinic signboard (63) and the old notice on the kitchen door (67). Every one is angled away, dusty, glared out or shot from behind. In 27 the horror is the neatness of the columns, not the words.

6. The husband exists only in photographs. Shots 24 and 25, as faded twenty-year-old prints with period colour. He never appears in the present day.

7. The village is pleasant. Daylight, tamarind trees, a teashop. No haze, no fog, no ominous grade. The film is about a monster in a perfectly nice place.
